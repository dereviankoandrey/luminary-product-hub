Can We Trust LLMs to Underwrite Our Deals?

The arXiv paper that proved AI agents use language models to manipulate each other has a direct parallel in real estate underwriting. Here's the interactive evidence.

Produced September 6, 2026 • Luminary Bot Ventures
The core finding: A recent arXiv paper ("LLMs as a Cognitive Virus," by Jobbings & Gorman) demonstrated how language models — when prompted to predict human text distribution — can generate content that propagates and replicates through agent ecosystems without regard for accuracy, truth, or safety. The model optimizes the wrong metric and goes rogue.

The same problem exists in real estate underwriting. When an AI tool is told to "produce a profitable deal analysis," it can hallucinate inflated numbers — not because anyone lied to it, but because its training distribution favors optimistic outputs that sound confident. This paper explains why and gives us a framework for understanding it.

The Paper's Warning, Applied to Real Estate

Jobbings & Gorman show that a language model trained as a general text predictor — given no explicit instruction to mislead — can still evolve behavior that produces misleading, replicating outputs with no self-correction mechanism. The root cause: optimization on the wrong signal (mimic training distribution) instead of truth-seeking. This is what happens when real estate investors use AI tools for underwriting. They're not being lied to — they're getting exactly what any unbounded ML system does best: confident, plausible-sounding output that matches a learned pattern. The problem isn't intent. It's optimization target mismatch, and the Cognitive Virus paper proves it happens naturally.

What You Can Actually Do About This

Option 1: Use deterministic tools (Luminary approach)

  • Every calculation is a visible formula — nothing to hide in model weights
  • Analytical rigor: CAP rate, DSCR, cash-on-cash return all computed from transparent inputs
  • Versus black-box AI: if the AI says "profitable," ask show me your math. The answer is always the same — there is no math to show. Just a probability distribution over text that happened to include dollar signs and the word "profit."

Option 2: If you must use AI, demand auditability

  • No black box — require tools that show their calculation steps
  • Cross-verify every key number against independent sources (rent comps from Zillow, cap rates from Crexi, costs from RSMeans data)
  • If an AI tool can't produce a line-by-line breakdown it would also accept from you, don't trust its output
